Abstract Lung cancer is a primary cause of cancer death globally and accounts for one of the highest rates of mortality among all cancer types. The significant fatality rate from lung cancer is predominantly due to late-stage discovery of the disease and lack of immediate medical intervention. Therefore, efficient and accurate detection of lung cancer plays a vital role in improving a patient’s chance of survival. The typical manual workflow used to diagnose lung cancer from CT scans is not only slow but also prone to human error and produces results with low repeatability, especially in healthcare systems with limited resources. Therefore, in this project we aim to provide a solution to automate the detection of lung cancer as well as report generation using a vision-language framework.
We introduce a custom CNN architecture for classifying lung CT scans. The network efficiently learns image features through multiple levels of convolutions and pooling to generate optimal classifications of the input images. Alongside this, we propose to leverage a pre-trained GPT-based LLM to automate the report generation workflow. By finetuning this LLM with the output of our CNN, we hope to facilitate vision-language fusion by allowing image-to-text prompt options.
Methods: In this paper, we design, train, and evaluate our custom CNN to classify images as positive or negative for lung cancer. In future work, we will extract features from our image classification network and perform vision-language fusion through the LLM to generate unique reports. We believe that AI can help automate tedious tasks present in medical imaging workflows. Our proposed framework aims to reduce redundancy and provide an extensible and modular solution to healthcare providers. We demonstrate the efficacy of our CNN by
training and testing on publicly available lung cancer datasets. Our model reaches an accuracy of a comprehensive, multi-modal diagnostic system that promises to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce variability, and enhance efficiency in clinical settings.
Keywords: Generative AI, medical imaging, LLMs, X-ray, CT scan, MRI scan, computer vision, diagnostics
I. Introduction
Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of death across the world, killing more than 1.8 million people every year. While technology has provided us many amazing tools to view the inside of our bodies, like CT scans, much of the manual interpretation is still done by humans. Diagnosis of chest X-rays is a highly manual, laborious task that is prone to high intervariability and errors. Distinguishing between normal, benign and malignant cases is a challenging task.
Much work has been done in implementing AI in medical imaging pipelines to reduce manual workload and improve performance. Vision-based tasks like image classification are well suited for traditional computer vision models. But we can pair vision with large language models (LLMs) to automatically generate diagnostic reports. There have been some solutions that aim to combine both of these modalities, but not many provide a unified solution.
In this project, we aim to provide a solution combining a custom CNN model to classify CT scans along with a GPT-based LLM to generate the reports. We intend to architect a system to assist doctors in not only providing accurate classifications but also rich context-aware diagnostic reports. The first phase will provide us a strong CNN-based architecture to classify images. The next step would be to work on vision-language fusion.

A. Research Gap
Medical AI lung cancer classification using convolutional neural networks is one of the fastest-growing areas. However, there are still several fundamental challenges limiting its potential practical implementation in clinical settings. The main limitations are the dependence on resources like LIDC-IDRI and LUNA16, which are widely used and hence inherently limiting in terms of diversity. These datasets often represent specific imaging conditions (e.g., CT scans acquired under controlled settings) and may not capture the variability of populations, imaging protocols, or disease presentations found in real-world clinical settings. This lack of diversity results in models that struggle to generalize when applied to heterogeneous clinical data, leading to unreliable performance across different patient populations. Moreover, CNNs are largely static-image-driven, thus limiting their ability to analyze longitudinal data that are critical for tracking disease progression. Such temporal analysis is especially important in lung cancer because the changes in size, shape, or density of a nodule can be extremely useful in diagnosis and prognosis. In addition to the technical barriers, opacity to CNN models is a major stumbling block for clinical adoption. Despite their ability to produce high diagnostic accuracy, their "black box" nature prevents clinicians from understanding how the models actually arrive at specific predictions. This lack of interpretability creates a trust gap in that clinicians are unlikely to rely on systems without having clear, actionable insights into their decision-making processes. Addressing these limitations will require substantial advancements in dataset development, model design, and interpretability-focused research.
The field of fully automated report generation with GPT-based LLMs also displays stark limitations, especially for taking multimodal inputs in such a way that combines information from visual data as well as textual information. Of course, LLMs like GPT have had astonishing fluency and coherence in generating human-like text; their actual clinical utility is limited by the inability to fully integrating with image-derived data seamlessly. Current implementations tend to separate the image analysis and text generation as separate processes, so minimal interaction is provided between the two. In turn, generated reports capture some textual patterns but cannot properly contextualize critical findings from the images from CT or X-rays, thus reducing the reports' diagnostic accuracy and relevance. For example, although these systems may perform well at summarizing pre-annotated textual datasets, they tend to miss subtle image features that play a crucial role in identifying conditions such as early-stage lung cancer. Furthermore, the training datasets used, such as CheXpert and IU X-Ray, are often prepared for narrow tasks and lack multimodal annotations that capture the interplay between imaging findings and clinical narratives. This poses challenges in extending these models to broader clinical applications or more complex imaging modalities, such as MRI or PET scans, that require a deeper understanding of both visual and contextual data. Moreover, these systems fail to account for the hierarchical nature of medical reporting, where a combination of descriptive observations, differential diagnoses, and actionable recommendations must be seamlessly integrated. These gaps highlight the critical necessity of innovative approaches to multimodal learning in which text and image data are jointly analyzed, culminating in comprehensive, clinically meaningful reports.
Vision-language fusion, which seeks the combination of visual and textual data for enhanced diagnostics, represents a promising but, however, underdeveloped domain in medical AI. Most of the current methods used in vision-language integration are based on rather basic methods, such as output concatenation from independent vision and language models, failing to capture the rich multidimensional relationships between image features and textual descriptions. This simplistic integration often results in systems that can perform basic tasks, such as captioning an image with general observations, but lack the depth needed for complex diagnostic scenarios, where subtle visual patterns must be linked to specific clinical findings. For example, identifying a small nodule in a CT scan and correlating it with potential malignancy based on patient history requires a much deeper fusion of data modalities than current systems can achieve. Another significant gap in this area is the limited ability of models to generalize across different medical domains or imaging modalities. Systems trained on chest X-rays, for example, often fail miserably when applied to CT scans or MRI data, largely due to the lack of strong transfer learning techniques that could adapt models to new tasks or datasets without significant retraining. Moreover, vision-language fusion systems often cannot be used to incorporate clinical metadata, such as patient demographics or previous medical history, which could significantly enhance their diagnostic capabilities. Addressing these limitations will require not only advanced fusion techniques, such as multimodal transformers or attention-based architectures, but also the development of datasets that capture the complexity and diversity of real-world clinical scenarios.
Scalability and computational efficiency are additional challenges to the deployment of AI systems in medical imaging. State-of-the-art deep learning models are powerful but computationally expensive, requiring high-end hardware such as GPUs or TPUs, significant memory, and energy resources. Such requirements make them infeasible for many healthcare environments, especially low-resource settings where such infrastructure is not available. The problem of scalability is exacerbated when dealing with large, high-resolution medical imaging datasets, where processing time and computational costs can become prohibitive. Another significant challenge is the flexibility of existing models to adapt to new imaging technologies or clinical workflows. For example, a new generation of high-resolution CT scanners often requires the retraining or redesign of existing models, a time-consuming and resource-intensive process. These challenges compound with the need to comply with regulatory requirements and ensure patient data privacy that often require additional layers of security and auditing, further increasing the computational burden. These scalability issues will be overcome with innovation in model optimization: light architectures that operate well on limited hardware and advance in transfer learning, so that models can easily adapt to new datasets or imaging modalities without considerable retraining.
Future research has to focus on the development of robust, adaptable, and efficient AI systems tailored to the complexities of medical diagnostics. The generation of diverse, high-quality datasets reflecting variability in the real world would be critical for lung cancer classification, as well as designing models that incorporate longitudinal data to track disease progression over time. In generating reports automatically, advanced multimodal learning techniques will integrate visual and textual data to enable comprehensive, context-aware diagnostic reports. Vision-language fusion systems could benefit from newer architectures like transformers designed with cross-modal understanding in mind and better suited to link features of an image with the insights textual. Finally, scalability is going to depend on building computationally efficient models and methods for rapid adaptation to new imaging technologies. These advances will not only enhance the accuracy and dependability of AI systems but also make them universally acceptable in different healthcare environments, leading to improved diagnostic results and patient care around the globe.

A. Definitions
1) Lung Cancer Diagnosis: The process of identifying and classifying lung cancer through medical imaging, typically using CT scans, X-rays, or MRI scans. The diagnosis involves detecting abnormalities and determining whether they are benign or malignant.
2) CT Scan: A medical imaging technique that uses X-rays to create detailed cross-sectional images of the body, widely used to detect lung cancer and other diseases.
3) Benign: A non-cancerous tumor or lesion. Benign growths typically do not spread to other parts of the body.
4) Malignant: A cancerous tumor or growth that can invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body, making it more dangerous than benign tumors.
5) Convolutional Neural Network (CNN): A class of deep neural networks that are particularly powerful for analyzing visual data. CNNs are commonly used for image classification tasks in computer vision applications, such as lung cancer detection.
6) Large Language Model (LLM): A type of machine learning model trained to understand and generate human language. GPT-based models are examples of LLMs used for generating text based on given input data.
7) Automated Report Generation: The process of using AI to generate written diagnostic reports from medical data, such as CT scan images. In this project, LLMs are used to generate contextual, accurate reports based on the features extracted by the CNN model.

V. Methodology
The methodology outlines the step-by-step approach to designing, developing, and evaluating the AI-driven framework for lung cancer detection and automated report generation. The framework integrates a custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for image classification. The key stages of this methodology are as follows:
A. Data Collection and Preprocessing
The first step involves gathering a comprehensive dataset of lung CT scan images and diagnostic reports. For training and testing the CNN, high-quality annotated datasets such as IQOTHNCCD-LUNG-CANCER-DATASET or other publicly available datasets are used.
Preprocessing Steps:
1) Color Correction (RGB Conversion): Since OpenCV reads images in BGR format by default, we convert the color image from BGR to RGB using cv2.cvtColor(). This ensures that the color channels are correctly aligned for further processing or visualization.
2) Resizing: The image is resized to a fixed size of img_size by img_size (e.g., 256x256 pixels). Resizing is crucial for ensuring that all images in the dataset have the same dimensions, which is a requirement for feeding them into neural networks.
3) Grayscale Conversion: The resized RGB image is converted to grayscale using cv2.cvtColor(). Grayscale images simplify the task by reducing the number of channels from 3 (RGB) to 1, which can be beneficial for certain image classification tasks, especially when color information is not crucial.
4) Gaussian Blur: Gaussian blur is applied to smooth the image and reduce noise, which helps improve the performance of subsequent steps like thresholding and feature extraction. The kernel size is set to (5, 5), which controls the degree of smoothing.
5) Thresholding (Otsu's Method): Otsu's thresholding method is used to segment the image into foreground and background. It automatically calculates an optimal threshold value to separate the pixels based on intensity. The output is a binary image where the foreground (potential cancerous regions) is distinguished from the background.
6) Normalization: The thresholded image is normalized by dividing all pixel values by 255.0, converting the pixel values to a range of [0, 1]. This step helps standardize the data and ensures that all input values are on the same scale.
7) Erosion and Dilation (Morphological Operations): Erosion is applied to remove small white regions (noise) from the binary image, which can help in reducing irrelevant details. Dilation is then applied to enhance the remaining features, making them more prominent for further analysis.

B. Custom CNN Architecture Design
The CNN model we're using follows a standard CNN architecture with two convolutional layers, followed by max-pooling, flattening, and fully connected layers. The model is designed to classify lung CT scan images into three categories: Normal, Benign, and Malignant.
Key Steps in CNN Design:
1) Convolution Layers: Learn features (edges, textures, patterns) from the images.
2) Max Pooling: Reduce the spatial dimensions to lessen the computational cost.
3) Flattening: Convert the feature maps into a 1D array for classification.
4) Dense Layers: Process the learned features and output the classification probabilities using softmax.
Overall Architecture: The input consists of CT scan images of lung tissues (resized to a uniform size). The network comprises Conv2D + ReLU + MaxPooling blocks (x2), followed by flattening and dense layers for classification. The output produces three classes (Normal, Benign, Malignant).

C. Training the CNN Model
The CNN will be trained on the labeled CT scan dataset using the following configuration:
1) Loss Function (sparse_categorical_crossentropy): Sparse Categorical Crossentropy is used as the loss function since this is a multi-class classification problem where the target labels are integers (not one-hot encoded). This loss function is appropriate when there are more than two classes and the model's output is a probability distribution over all the classes (generated by the softmax activation in the output layer).
2) Optimizer (adam): Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) is used as the optimizer because it combines the benefits of both RMSProp and Momentum. It adapts the learning rate for each parameter, which helps the model converge faster and more effectively. Adam is a popular choice for training deep learning models as it tends to perform well across a variety of tasks.
3) Metrics (accuracy): Accuracy is used as the evaluation metric during training. This will measure the percentage of correct predictions made by the model across all test samples. Since it's a classification task with multiple classes, accuracy gives us a straightforward measure of how well the model is performing.
D. Language Model Integration (Future Stage)

The language model will be fine-tuned using a medical corpus of lung cancer reports, allowing it to generate detailed and context-aware reports.

VIII. Architecture
The architecture of the lung cancer detection and automated report generation system is designed to ensure a seamless flow of data through its various components, combining image processing, classification, and report generation. The system leverages a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for lung cancer detection and integrates a Large Language Model (LLM) for generating context-aware diagnostic reports. The architecture includes the following key components:
Input CT Scan Images: CT scan images are uploaded by the radiologist or healthcare professional. The system supports image formats such as .jpg, .png, or .dicom.
Preprocessing Module: Image resizing resizes all images to a uniform dimension (e.g., 256x256 pixels); Gaussian blur is applied to reduce noise in the images; color images are converted to grayscale if required; the image is segmented using Otsu's thresholding method and normalized to a range of [0, 1]; and morphological operations (erosion and dilation) enhance the image for further analysis.
CNN Model: Convolutional layers extract low- and high-level features from the preprocessed images; pooling layers reduce the spatial dimensions of feature maps; fully connected layers flatten the feature maps and map them to the classification probabilities. The output classifies the image into one of three categories: Normal, Benign, or Malignant.
Feature Extraction and Integration: Extracts the output of the CNN's feature maps and maps them to the input space of the LLM. Combines image-derived features with optional textual prompts for generating detailed diagnostic reports.
LLM-Based Report Generator: Uses a GPT-based model fine-tuned on medical datasets to generate a comprehensive diagnostic report. Incorporates information such as classification results, tumor characteristics (size, shape, location), and clinical recommendations.
Output Layer: Displays the class (Normal, Benign, or Malignant) predicted by the CNN, and provides a structured, human-readable diagnostic report containing findings and recommendations.
The architecture ensures seamless integration of CNN-based classification and LLM-based report generation to automate lung cancer detection and diagnostic reporting. It is designed to be modular, scalable, and adaptable, supporting future enhancements such as the addition of new imaging modalities and real-time decision support features. This architecture provides a robust framework for improving clinical workflows, enhancing diagnostic accuracy, and reducing the workload on radiologists.

The AI-driven lung cancer detection system has shown promising results in the classification of CT scan images into three categories: Normal, Benign, and Malignant. Furthermore, the system was able to generate automated reports based on the classification results, providing clear, actionable insights for healthcare professionals.
While the current system successfully classifies lung CT scans using a CNN model, several areas for improvement and future development include:
1) Integration with Large Language Models (LLMs): Future work will focus on incorporating LLMs (e.g., OpenLLM, GPT-3 or GPT-4) to generate more detailed, context-aware diagnostic reports, providing richer insights based on classification results.
2) Handling Multiple Imaging Modalities: Expanding the system to process X-rays, MRI scans, and other imaging types will make it more versatile, allowing comprehensive cancer detection across different imaging modalities.
3) Real-Time Decision Support: The system can be enhanced to provide real-time recommendations based on classification results, assisting clinicians with immediate next steps in diagnosis or treatment.
